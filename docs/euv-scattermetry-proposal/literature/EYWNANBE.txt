EUV-angle resolved scatter (EUV-ARS): a new tool for the
characterization of nanometre structures
Analı ́a Ferna ́ndez Herreroa, Heiko Mentzela, Victor Soltwischa, Sina Jaroslawzewa, Christian Laubisa, and Frank Scholzea
aPhysikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt, Abbestr. 2-12, 10587 Berlin, Germany
ABSTRACT
The advance of the semiconductor industry requires new metrology methods, which can deal with smaller and more complex nanostructures. Particularly for inline metrology a rapid, sensitive and non destructive method is needed. Small angle X-ray scattering under grazing incidence has already been investigated for this application and delivers significant statistical information which tracks the profile parameters as well as their variations, i.e. roughness. However, it suffers from the elongated footprint at the sample. The advantage of EUV radiation, with its longer wavelengths, is that larger incidence angles can be used, resulting in a significant reduction of the beam footprint. Targets with field sizes of 100 μm and smaller are accessible with our experimental set-up. We present a new experimental tool for the measurement of small structures based on the capabilities of soft X-ray and EUV scatterometry at the PTB soft X-ray beamline at the electron storage ring BESSY II. PTB’s soft X-ray radiometry beamline uses a plane grating monochromator, which covers the spectral range from 0.7 nm to 25 nm and was especially designed to provide highly collimated radiation. An area detector covers the scattered radiation from a grazing exit angle up to an angle of 30◦ above the sample horizon and the fluorescence emission can be detected with an energy dispersive X-ray silicon drift detector. In addition, the sample can be rotated and linearly moved in vacuum. This new set-up will be used to explore the capabilities of EUV-scatterometry for the characterization of nanometre-sized structures.
Keywords: EUV, soft X-ray, CD-metrology, scatterometry, composition analysis
1. INTRODUCTION
The decreasing sizes and the increasing complexity of the nanostructures in the last decades have motivated the development of new metrology solutions.1 As the critical dimensions shrink, any imperfection of the structures even plays a more important role over the total structure during the process control.2 A reliable measurement technique must be sensitive to tiny variations of small features. Scanning techniques, with their advantage of measuring in real space, have been very useful for the determination of the critical dimensions of the features but for relevant statistical information this procedure is tedious, and its applicability for the determination of parameters from complex structures is limited.
Scattering metrology methods play a very important role in the development of new tools because they allow a rapid and destruction-free investigation of nanostructures, and are sensitive to 3D structures. Scattering techniques can be based on transmission3, 4 or reflection, being the latest a more suitable technique for the investigation of the nanostructured surfaces, due to the enhancement of the signal when an incidence angle close to the critical angle is used. Grazing incidence small angle X-ray scattering (GISAXS) is a well established technique for the analysis of nanostructures,5, 6 with a high surface sensitivity. However, one of the major drawbacks of this technique is the rather large footprint of the beam compared to the sample size, which leads to a contribution to the scattering signal from the surrounding area. Although this issue has already been addressed for periodic nanostructures in a periodic-and-azimuthal-rotated surrounding area,7 a more general solution is desirable. Another approach would be to use large incidence wavelengths, allowing for larger incidence angles, leading to a decrease of the footprint of the beam in the beam direction without compromising the high surface sensitiveness of the technique. However compared to GISAXS the resolution for additional superstructures, along the grating lines, are of a few nanometre while in GISAXS are in the sub-micrometre range. However with every scaling step the performance of the low nanometre features do not just depend on the dimensional parameters but also on its composition. A complementary measurement method of the elemental composition, which does not endanger the sample, must be considered for a complete analysis. Grazing incidence techniques are not just relevant in scatterometry, but also useful for material-sensitive analysis.11, 12 Grazingincidence X-ray Fluorescence (GIXRF) is an angular and energy dependent technique which makes use of the modulation of the fluorescence intensity with the X-ray standing wave field, closely related with the sampled structure.13 The combination of the scattering with the fluorescence analyses yields information about the dimensional structure and its composition.
We show a new measurement tool to be placed in the soft X-ray beamline at PTB in BESSY II, which covers the spectral range from 0.7 nm to 25 nm. It consists of a chamber with a CCD area detector attached which accepts the scattered radiation up to an angle of 30◦. Inside the chamber the sample can be azimuthally rotated and moved towards the CCD plane in vacuum. Also, an energy dispersive X-ray silicon drift detector (SDD) can be introduced, which allow a complementary material-sensitive study and exploits the modulation through the X-rays standing wave field. The combination of both techniques will improve the characterization of nanostructured surfaces.
2. SOFT X-RAY SCATTEROMETER
Figure 1: Longitudinal cut of the EUV-angle resolved scatterometer. The dark red line indicates the photon beam travelling towards the sample. After the photon-beam-sample interaction, the scattering signal can be recorded by a CCD area detector.
The soft X-ray scatterometer is based in the work principles of grazing incidence small angle X-ray scattering and optimized to measure nanostructured surfaces. In figure 1 the complete installation can be observed, the incident beam comes from the left part and travel towards the sample, where it interacts. Then the scattered light is recorded by a CCD. This set-up is optimized to work in the soft X-ray energy range, the larger incidence wavelengths, compared to X-rays, allow steeper incidence angles without compromising the surface sensitivity of the technique. With larger grazing incidence angles, the beam footprint on the sample decreases, which may avoid an over-illumination in the beam direction while still obtaining relevant statistical information. The footprint of the beam in the beam direction varies with the incidence angle, αi, by the factor 1/ tan(αi). The incident photon beam has a size of 0.5 mm by 0.5 mm, and can be reduced by using a combination of pinholes, see light blue insets in figure 1, such that targets fields of 100 μm and smaller are accessible.
An adequate measurement tool must accomplish the challenge of getting the more and most accurate information in a short time. The accessible amount of information is critical for the reconstruction .14 Comparing to GISAXS, smaller sample-detector distances are needed to investigate comparable reciprocal space with EUV and soft Xray. The sample is placed inside a vacuum chamber at a distance of 40 mm to the detector and can be linearly displaced towards the CDD or perpendicular to it. These two displacement directions allow to analyse several points on the sample. The sample holder can be moved 20 mm towards or away from the detector and 100 mm

Scatterometry is not a direct measurement technique. The reconstruction of the profiles of the investigated nanostructures must be performed by solving the so-called inverse problem. The scattering intensities must be statistically significant for the analysis. The specular reflection from the incoming beam may have much larger intensity than the scattering phenomena to be analysed. For example, for the observation of the roughness-induced scattering, i.e. the diffuse background, which is much less intense than the specular reflection. Therefore a beamstop is placed before the detector, which allows larger exposure times without saturating the detector. The beamstop has a trident form because it is designed to eclipse the specular reflection, which is usually in the center of the CCD. However under certain structural parametric conditions it might be advisable to set the specular reflection at the edge of the chamber, for example for the analysis of larger q-ranges.
In the geometry described here for the EUV-ARS, with smaller sample-detector distance and larger incidence angles, the investigated qy ranges are comparable to those in GISAXS. However GISAXS allows resolution of the qx in the micrometre range while EUV allows the investigation of nanometre features. The relative orientation of the truncation rods influences the

scattering signal, therefore the φ-alignment is crucial for the understanding of the scattering pattern and reconstruction of the structures.
For the complementary characterization of the nanostructures a SDD detector is attached to the chamber, which allows an investigation of the element composition of the structures. The fluorescence intensity, with respect to the near field intensity distribution, is correlated to the depth distribution and mass density of the elements in the sample. The SDD detector is to be placed at 15◦ to the sample normal.
To summarize, with this set-up, angles of incidence up to 30 ◦ from the surface can be obtained. The sample can be azimuthally rotated and the distance between CCD and sample optimized. The combination of these degrees of freedom allows to span different frequencies of the reciprocal space of the elastic scattered vector. Inelastic scattering can give rise to a fluorescence signal that can be analysed with a SDD detector placed close to the normal direction of the sample.

The scattering pattern from this samples is given by the intersection of the Ewald sphere with the grating truncation rods, projected into the CCD plane. As it was mentioned in the previous section, the characterization of the nanostructures through their scattering pattern involves to solve the so-called inverse problem. In case of a grating, the main parameters, such as pitch, linewidth and height, must be considered together with the more specific ones: corner rounding, sidewall angle, roughness. This brings up to a minimum set of parameters that must be found. Although the pitch is related to the distance between the diffraction orders in qy, the others must be found in the reconstruction process. Therefore, the number of the observable regular diffraction orders n, usually used for the reconstruction, is critical for a good characterization of the nanostructures. The observable orders of diffraction depend on the reflectance of the structure, the geometrical constraints of the set-up and the pitch of the structure. 5. CONCLUSION
We have exposed the feasibility of a new measurement tool based on the necessities of process control of lithographic samples. This new tool exploits the advantages of grazing incidence techniques and allows the investigation of nanostructured surfaces by scatterometry and fluorescence. This scatterometry technique is similar to GISAXS but large incidence angles at larger wavelengths allow smaller footprints without compromising the surface sensitiveness. Similar qy ranges are obtained with both methods. However EUV scatterometry also allows the investigation of the nm-regime in the qx. The SDD attached to the scatterometer makes possible a complementary compositional analysis.21
