Abstract
Scatterometry is a fast, precise and low cost way to determine the mean pitch and dimensional parameters of periodic structures with lateral resolution of a few nanometer. It is robust enough for inline process control and precise and accurate enough for metrology measurements. Furthermore, scatterometry is a non-destructive technique capable of measuring buried structures, for example a grating covered by a thick oxide layer. As scatterometry is a non-imaging technique, mathematical modeling is needed to retrieve structural parameters that describe a surface. In this review, the three main steps of scatterometry are discussed: the data acquisition, the simulation of diffraction efficiencies and the comparison of data and simulations. First, the intensity of the diffracted light is measured with a scatterometer as a function of incoming angle, diffraction angle and/or wavelength. We discuss the evolution of the scatterometers from the earliest angular scatterometers to the new imaging scatterometers. The basic principle of measuring diffraction efficiencies in scatterometry has remained the same since the beginning, but the instrumental improvements have made scatterometry a state-of-the-art solution for fast and accurate measurements of nano-textured surfaces. The improvements include extending the wavelength range from the visible to the extreme ultra-violet range, development of Fourier optics to measure all diffraction orders simultaneously, and an imaging scatterometer to measure area of interests smaller than the spot size. Secondly, computer simulations of the diffraction efficiencies are discussed with emphasis on the rigorous coupled-wave analysis (RCWA) method. RCWA has, since the mid-1990s, been the preferred method for grating simulations due to the speed of the algorithms. In the beginning the RCWA method suffered from a very slow convergence rate, and we discuss the historical improvements to overcome this challenge, e.g. by the introduction of Li’s factorization rules and the introduction of the normal vector method. The third step is the comparison, where the simulated diffraction efficiencies are compared to the experimental data using an inverse modeling approach. We discuss both a direct optimization scheme using a differential evolution algorithm and a library search strategy where diffraction efficiences of expected structures are collected in a database. For metrology measurements two methods are described for estimating the uncertainty of the fitting parameters. The first method is based on estimating the confidence limits using constant chi square boundaries, which can easily be computed when using the library search strategy. The other method is based on calculating the covariances of all the free parameters using a least square optimization. Scatterometry is already utilized in the semiconductor industry for in-line characterization. However, it also has a large potential for other industrial sectors, including sectors making use of injection molding or roll-2-roll fabrication. Using the library search strategy, the comparison can be performed in ms, making in-line characterization possible and we demonstrate that scatterometry can be used for quality control of injection molded nano-textured plastic samples. With the emerging methods of highly parallel manufacturing of nano-textured devices, scatterometry has great potential to deliver a characterization method for in-line quality

1. Introduction
Diffraction is the phenomenon of light waves interacting with an object or a slit. If multiple objects or slits are periodically positioned, a so-called diffraction grating is formed, and interference of scattered light from these objects forms diffraction patterns. The properties of diffraction gratings were first described by Rittenhouse more than 200 years ago based on observations through a silk handkerchief [1]. Few years later, Thomas Young studied the diffraction orders and not only the specular reflection [2]. Diffraction gratings have since then played an important role in the understanding of physics and in the development of new technology including spectrometers [3], frequency stabilized lasers [4], and optical multiplexing devices [5]. However, the occurrence of diffraction is much older than mankind and advanced patterns leading to colorful iridescence are found on many animals including: beetles, butterflies, birds, and moths [6]. With modern technology and fabrication methods one can reproduce the fascinating structures from nature [7] or from an inverse design approach [8]. One example of newly developed structures are surfaces with structural colors [9], i.e. surfaces where diffraction or plasmonic effects [10, 11] are used to exhibit colors or color changing as sketched in figure 1. Some of the advantages of structural colors are that they are non-degradable [7] and that they can be fabricated without a paint step [12]. Other examples include super-hydrophobic surfaces inspired by the lotus leaf
[13] and hydrophilic surfaces known from the carnivorous pitcher plant [14], where it is used to form traps for insects. These new structures are often too small to be characterized with normal light microscopy techniques, including interference and confocal microscopy, due to the limited resolution of these systems. 2. Scatterometry setups
A wide range of scatterometry setups exist with some of the typical measurement geometries sketched in figure 4. In a fixed angle setup, figure 4(A), the incoming angle is kept fixed, while the detector is moved in the plane of the incoming beam to detect the diffracted beams. For a 2-theta setup, figure 4(B), the specular beam is detected as a function of the angle of the incoming beam. The specular beam is also detected in a spectroscopic setup, figure 4(C), but here as a function of wavelength. Using special optics, multiple diffraction orders can be measured simultaneously, as sketched in figure 4(D). All these systems, and some variations of them, will be discussed further in the present chapter. An overview of some of the scatterometer systems are also described by Raymond in [25, 26], but the systems have improved significantly in the last decade. 2.1. Angular scatterometers
The first applications of scatterometry for metrology measurements were demonstrated by Kleinknecht and Meier in 1978 where they monitored the etch rate in SiO2 and Si3N4 [34]. Two years later their setup was improved to include measurements of the linewidth on photomasks [35]. They used a static setup where both the incoming beam and the detector were kept at fixed angles, and thus, they were limited to measure the intensity of a single diffraction order. However, with no moving parts the system could monitor the etch rate in SiO2 and Si3N4 by measuring the intensity oscillations in the first order diffraction spot. By adding a second detector to measure the second order diffraction, more advanced measurements were possible. Scatterometers had for some time been available as they were used for measuring the scattering distribution to determine e.g. the surface roughness [36]. For this topic, the reader is referred to [37]. These scatterometers have a fixed angle of incidence, θi, and a moving detector to measure the angle-dependent scattering intensities. Despite the single moving arm, this type of scatterometer is also referred to as the fixed angle configuration. Such a setup can directly measure the pitch, d, of a periodic lattice using the grating equation

 The fixed angle scatterometers combined with the introduction of the method of rigorous coupled-wave analysis (RCWA) in the 1980s, which will be described further in chapter 3, matured the scatterometry method to determine dimensions of gratings. Scatterometry was demonstrated for measuring the line width [39, 40] and etch depth [41] of a grating. Despite the simplicity of the fixed angle scatterometer, it has proven to be a valuable tool for in situ characterization and for measurements on gratings with a pitch much longer than the wavelength of light. The pitch to wavelength ratio is a fundamental quantity in scatterometry for identifying the complexity of the light–matter interaction [42]. A full vectorial mathematical formulation describes the interaction over the whole wavelength range whereas scalar diffraction theory and renormalized scalar diffraction theory are adequately for pitch/wavelength ratios larger than 10 and 3, respectively. The grating period is resolved relative to the wavelength of the incoming light, and most gratings can therefore be said to have a long period when using deep ultra-violet [43] or extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light. Using EUV light with high spectral purity [44], profiles of multi-parameters gratings have been reconstructed [45], and the influence of line-edge roughness (LER) and linewidth roughness (LWR) of the gratings have been studied [46–48]. In a recent study, a fixed angle scatterometer was used to characterize injection molded microfluidic channels with a period of 5 μm [15]. The samples were fabricated in a transparent polymer and measured in a transmission configuration as sketched in figure 5. Using an Argon Ion laser operated at 488 nm, up to 8 diffraction orders could be measured as shown in figure 5(B). As there are three orders of magnitude difference in the intensity of the zeroth order and higher
order peaks, figure 5(C), it is necessary to have a detector system with a large dynamical range, e.g. by using variable gain settings on the photodetector or using a calibrated variable neutral density filter [49]. The signal noise can be significantly reduced by establishing lock-in detection, e.g. by mechanically chopping the light and use a lock-in amplifier. The input polarization state of the light was selected using a Berek’s variable wave plate and a polarizer. By measuring multiple diffraction orders at several incident angles and for both TE and TM polarization, Robert et al reconstructed the height, width and sidewall angle of a 1D grating with a period of 1 μm etched in Si [50]. By proper choice of their measurement points they could later reduce the number of measurements necessary to only 6 without sacrificing the accuracy [51]. A similar approach has also been used to characterize complex gratings with wavy sidewall profiles [52]. Also reconstruction of nanopillars positioned in a 2D grating with pitches of 780 nm has been demonstrated using a fixed angle system [53]. However, only diffraction orders in the plane of incidence was measured, and in order to increase the accuracy of the results, the sample was measured at two incidence angles and for both TE and TM polarized light. In general, the diffraction efficiencies of the nonzero orders have a higher sensitivity to changes in grating parameters, which increases the robustness of the fit to the experimental data. If possible, the -1st order should be included in the measurements, since this is the last order that remains and it tends to have higher diffraction efficiency than the other nonzero orders [52]. 2.2. Spectroscopic scatterometers
For robust reconstruction of the scatterometry measurements many individual data points are needed, as will be discussed in details in chapter 4. Instead of scanning the detector angle, one can instead scan the wavelength of the incoming light. Typically such a system is realized using a broad-band light source and filtering the light at the detector side, e.g. with a spectrometer. This is referred to as a spectroscopic scatterometry [61–63]. Studying the first (and higher) orders of the diffracted light is cumbersome as the diffraction angles also depend on the wavelength as observed from equation (5). Thus, typically one only studies the specular reflection, and in the simplest configuration, the incoming light and detector are positioned normally to the sample as sketched in figure 4(C). An advantage of the normal incidence configuration is that alignment is easy and the system is very robust to vibrations [17]. For a simple implementation of a spectroscopic scatterometer, a spectrometer can be built into a conventional microscope either by exchanging the camera, as illustrated in figure 6, or by introduction of a second beam splitter in the beam path [17]. The
advantage of having the scatterometer integrated in an optical microscope is that the area-of interest can readily be found and the sample can be brought in focus; two of the challenges for a non-imaging system. The data shown in figure 6 are from a measurement series of a 1D grating etched in a Si substrate. The ‘reference’ is obtained on a blank area on the same sample. Usually one would measure the ‘dark’ signal by blocking the light source. However, as the optical components in the microscope reflect the light, it is preferable to measure the ‘dark’ signal by guiding the light into an optical beam dump. Typical spectroscopic scatterometers analyzes an area equal to the illumination spot size. It means that an average value of the structures is found. Measurements on small areas are challenging due to chromatic aberrations in the optical components and the fact that one typically would prefer to avoid hard focusing, such that simulations of the diffraction efficiencies can be performed in the paraxial regime. With a novel design, we have demonstrated that using imaging scatterometry, one can measure on small areas (say 5 μm×5 μm) with nm resolution [64]. With imaging scatterometry a series of images at different wavelengths is acquired to build a multi-spectral image as sketched in figure 7(A). At each wavelength, a reference image, a dark image and an image of the sample are acquired and used to calculate the diffraction efficiency for a single pixel or multiple pixel binned together using equation (3). Binning is where multiple pixels are put together, e.g. 8×8, to form a new pixel and it reduces the noise. The diffraction efficiencies are calculated one pixel at a time and combined to form a full image. The wavelength dependent diffraction efficiencies for a single pixel can be extracted, as sketched in figure 7(B), and used for the scatterometry reconstruction. 2.3. Other scatterometers
Using a goniometer is a slow technique for measuring multiple diffraction orders, and especially for 2D gratings with out-of-plane diffraction it is challenging to perform the measurements. Instead several systems have been designed to measure multiple diffraction orders simultaneously. In the simplest form, multiple diffraction orders can be obtained simultaneously using a white sheet of paper and a camera [70] or a diffuse hemispherical screen [71]. With a smart design of lenses, optical Fourier transform scatterometry has been demonstrated for dimensional measurement [72, 73] and LER and LWR [74] of gratings. The same system has also been demonstrated for measuring the periods of 2D gratings without having to align the sample [75]. Optical Fourier Transform is based on a special set of lenses that projects all light in the same direction to a single point in the back-focal plane. Instruments having a collection angle up to 88° are commercially available. Also scatterfield microscopy, the name being from combining scatterometry and bright-field microscopy, as developed by Silver et al has been demonstrated to extend the limits of optical metrology [76, 77]. In scatterfield microscopy selected components of the scattered field are viewed and analyzed at a time by optical engineering of the illumination and imaging path in the conjugate back focal plane. Using e.g. a digital micro-mirror device the light can quickly be modified for scatterfield microscopy. In coherent Fourier scatterometry [78–81], a coherent light source is focused on the sample using a high NA objective. The high NA objective is also used to collect the diffracted light, and for large pitch
gratings (relative to the wavelength), several diffraction orders are captured simultaneously. By scanning the illumination spot over a grating period the intensities and phase differences of the overlapping orders can be retrieved and used for reconstruction of the sample parameters [81]. It has been shown that for some gratings, e.g. when the wavlength is less than half the grating pitch, the Coherent Fourier scatterometry is more sensitive for the grating shape parameters than inchorent scatterometry [79]. However, it is important to have knowledge of the incident electromagnetic field and any distortions, e.g. aberration effects in the lenses, of the system. The amplitude and phase information of the incident field can for instance be measured using a Shack–Hartmann Sensor as described in [79]. More information can be retrieved by measuring the individual phases, instead of just the phase differences, of the scattered waves by combining coherent Fourier scatterometry with interferometry [80]. Also a white light interferometric Fourier scatterometer has been demonstrated for measuring the basic grating parameters [82]. An overview of some of the scatterometry techniques and methods for reference measurements are summarized in table 1. In general, it is preferentially to have as many independent diffraction efficiencies as possible to achieve a robust result in the inverse modeling. When using a setup with a coherent light source all measurements are correlated to the optical properties of the materials at this specific wavelength. It is therefore beneficial to use either multiple coherent or a broad band light source to avoid all measurements to rely on single optical parameters.

3.1. Scalar diffraction theory
For simple 1D gratings, e.g. gratings with vertical sidewalls or sinusoidal gratings, one can use the theory of scalar diffraction theory to calculate the diffraction efficiencies [83, 84]. It has been claimed that scalar diffraction theory only applies to systems where the wavelength is much smaller than the grating pitch (λ/ d<0.1) [85, 86]. However, as shown by Harvey et al, by using a non-paraxial formulation of the scalar diffraction theory, the range of parameters can be greatly expanded [87]. 3.2. Rigorous coupled-wave analysis
RCWA is a computational effective method for calculation of diffraction efficiencies. The method is based on simplifying periodic structures by a staircase approximation using rectangular boxes as sketched in figure 8(B). In this section we will discuss the most important equations used in modern RCWA algorithms. In order to set up a RCWA simulation, it is necessary to find some representative parameters to describe the grating. Some typical parameters to describe a grating is the pitch as indicated in figure 9(A), and the height, width and sidewall angle as indicated in figure 9(B). For more advanced models the rounding of corners can be introduced by including two more grating parameters as indicated in figure 9(C). More options include using an asymmetrical model, adding a thin layer or stacking multiple gratings. A predecessor to RCWA was presented by Kogelnik in 1969 and used for calculation of diffraction efficiencies of thick hologram gratings [88]. In the following decades, the theory was improved in a number of small steps to include slicing of structures, surface relief structures, 2D surface relief structures and anisotropic gratings [89–91]. However, the implementations were not stable for high-aspect surface relief structures. A stable implementation for TE polarization was first presented by Moharam and Gaylord in 1995 [92]. However, the RCWA method described in this work suffered from a very slow TM polarized field convergence rate and did only work for binary gratings. At the same time they also published an article that made it possible to use RCWA for multilevel gratings [93]. The origin of the slow convergence rate of TM polarized light compared to TE polarized light remained unclear until two groups found that by reformulating the eigenproblem of RCWA, the TM convergence rate were highly improved [94, 95], and Li found that the poor convergence was due to wrong treatment of the permittivity for line gratings in truncated Fourier space [96]. Li’s factorization rules for obtaining faster convergence can be described by using the electric displacement field, D=ε0εΕ, where ε0ε is the permittivity and is E the electrical field. The stable and efficient implementation of the RCWA presented in this review always converges towards the diffracted field amplitudes. 3.3. RCWA solvers
There are a number of commercial available RCWA solvers aimed at different market segments. LightTrans GmbH is selling the VirtualLab software [105], which includes a RCWA solver as one of several solvers for calculating the electromagnet field propagation through optical elements. This software is aimed at advanced optical engineers that need to go beyond ray tracing in the design of optical setups. S4 is a free RCWA solver made at Stanford University [104]; the software includes several different implementation of the RCWA algorithm for simulating electromagnetic propagation through periodic
structures. The software has a text only user interface and is aimed at researchers that has time to compile and update the C/C++ source code as new progress is reported in literature. GD-Calc is a commercial RCWA solver from KJ Innovation that can be used within the Matlab programming environment [106]. The user has to write Matlab text file that describe the geometry and the materials within the periodic unit to be used for calculation of the electromagnetic field or diffraction efficiencies using GD-Calc. The software is aimed at researchers that prefer to use the Matlab environment for their work. InFoScat is a commercial software package from Image Metrology that includes a RCWA solver together with a module for the inverse problem solving using a library approach [107]. The software has a user friendly interface in which the user can construct gratings without the need to do any programming. The calculated diffraction efficiencies or ellipsometric parameters can be stored in a database, which afterwards can be loaded directly into the comparison module for the inverse problem solving to find the best set of parameters to describe the measured surface. The software is aimed for industrial use in a production and research environments that require a userfriendly and flexible solution. Gsolver is a commercial RCWA solver from Grating Solver Development Company [108]. The software has a user interface from which the user easily can build binary gratings, but the construction of multilevel gratings is cumbersome. The software also uses a very old implementation of the RCWA algorithm that has poor convergence, especially for 2D grating structures. The software is aimed for research and development engineers requiring an easy way for calculating binary gratings.
3.4. Other modeling methods
Other popular modeling methods include the FEM and finite-difference time-domain (FDTD). With FEM the structures are drawn graphically and then divided into a mesh as sketched in figure 8(C). Maxwell’s equations are solved for each point in the mesh with a computational solver program. Popular programs for finite-element analysis include COMSOL Multiphysics and JCMWave, with the first one being more general and the latter optimized for simulations of electromagnetic waves. FEM is very good at handling complex structures as one can use computer aided drawing (CAD) for designing the models. However, the computation time is much longer for such simulations, and when other options are available, we suggest using those instead [109]. In FDTD the E- and H-fields are calculated and advanced in small time steps. FDTD can handle multiple frequencies simultaneously and is thus best suited for spectroscopic scatterometry. On the other hand, in

Another approach is to build a database based on empirical data, where one sample, or a set of samples, is used as a ‘golden standard’ for all other measurements. This method is for instance useful for quality control of fabricated structures. To build a reliable database based on empirical data one needs to measure the diffraction efficiencies of reference samples and characterize the reference samples using another type of instrument. Because of the simplicity of using empirical models, it is widely used in industry and is a powerful method. The different techniques are summarized in table 1. Scalar diffraction and RCWA are the fastest methods, but is to some extend limited in the complexity of the input models. The FEM and FDTD methods can handle very advanced grating geometries, but the computation speed is much slower, and the code might need a supercomputer to run. Especially for spectroscopic systems, the speed of the algorithm is very important, as many wavelengths for each set of grating parameters have to be calculated.

4.1. Direct optimization
For simple geometries, or when having excessive amounts of computer power, a direct optimization approach can be used. Based on an initial guess by the user, all the free parameters are optimized using e.g. a differential evolution algorithm [114]. As an example we study a periodic array of microfluidic channels as described in [15]. In equations (6)(8) the two free parameters, the height and the filling factor, are optimized with respect to data acquired by an angular scatterometer. Convergence between experimental data, shown in figures 5(B) and (C), and the fit is found after less than 1000 iterations, see figure 11(A), with a computation time of less than 100 ms on a normal desktop computer. The best fit using scalar diffraction theory, shown in figure 11(B), is obtained for the parameters h=88.3 nm and w=436 nm for the height and the
width of the channels, respectively. Using a metrology AFM for reference measurements the height has been measured to (89.6±1.2) nm and the width estimated to larger than 454 nm [15]. For validation of the scalar diffraction theory, the diffraction efficiencies simulated using RCWA with the same parameters are plotted in the same figure. With RCWA it is furthermore found that the differences in diffraction efficiencies between straight sidewalls and sidewall angles tilted up to 15° are less than 1%. The direct optimization approach can also be used for more complex grating geometries where simulations are performed using RCWA or FEM. For samples with many free parameters and excessive computer power this approach might be advantageous. However, often one will look for a faster method for the inverse modeling, and in that case the library search method is a good approach.

4.3. Library search
In scatterometry, a library search approach is widely used for solving the inverse problem [25, 62]. For library search the chi-square value is computed for all elements in a pre-generated database. The database can contain thousands of scattering intensity profiles for different structures, and it is effectively only limited by the available time for generation of the database, which can be of the order of hours on a normal desktop computer. An example of experimental data and a best fit found using a library search is shown in figure 12(A). It can seem cumbersome to first generate a database and then perform the comparison. However, as the library search is an extremely fast algorithm, typically only a few ms, this approach is well suited for direct feed-back, e.g. when used in a production line. Another benefit of the library search algorithm is that it finds the global minimum within the pre-defined parameter range, which can be a challenge when using the direct optimization [117]. To increase the resolution one can subsequently perform a direct optimization with the values found using the library search as the initial parameters [118]. Instead of a pre-generated database, it has also been shown that a simple model based on e.g. scalar diffraction theory can be used for the initial guess, and subsequently a local optimization using RCWA simulations to increase the accuracy of the fit [42]. One will always find a ‘best fit’ when using the library search approach. Here the user has to be aware that if the true value is outside the pre-generated database, the given solution from the library search is not valid. And worse, even a faulty pre-generated database, where say wrong optical constants have been used, will give out an answer. Often these errors can be spotted by a skilled operator by comparing the best fit to the experimental values ‘by eye’. A more quantitative

4.5. Instrument uncertainties
For metrology measurements the scatterometer has to be calibrated and the sources of errors should be known [42, 111, 119, 120]. The most important sources of errors, including both random and systematic uncertainty contributions, for a scatterometer instrument are
(1) Detector noise.
(2) Light fluctuations.
(3) Long term stability of the light source.
(4) Polarization of the light.
(5) Incident angle.
(6) Wavelength of the detected light.
(7) Orientation of the sample.
(8) Light losses in the system.
(9) Incomplete collection of the scattered light.
The first two sources are random errors (Type A). They can be reduced by collecting more light, either by increasing the integration time or averaging of data sets. The analysis of random errors in scatterometry experiments is handled very well by the least square analysis. The remaining sources lead to systematic errors (type B) in the scatterometry measurements and obtaining more data does not help to reduce these errors. Systematic errors are not corrected by the least square analysis. Therefore, it is important to identify the sources of these errors and minimize them when possible. As an example, the long term stability of the light source can be monitored by a secondary detector, and all data can then be corrected in the postprocessing. The instrument uncertainties should be combined with the uncertainties related to the mathematically modeling and the input parameters to this, e.g. the optical properties of the used materials, to set up a full uncertainty budget. In the joint research project ‘Metrology of small structures for the manufacturing of electronic and optical devices’ part of the European Metrology Research Programme, the National Metrology Institutes from Germany (PTB) and Denmark (DFM) developed a full uncertainty budget for scatterometry and ellipsometry [121]. Traceable
measurements with a standard uncertainty of less than 1 nm were demonstrated in the project.
5. Industrial use
The first applications of scatterometry in the semiconductor industry were monitoring of etch processes [34] and measurements on integrated circuit masks in various stages of the production [35]. Using a simple hemispherical detector, Hatab et al measured the trench depth of dynamic random access memory devices in 1995. A study by Lee et al showed that in situ quantitative information of the topography of gratings could be retrieved by monitoring the specularly reflected light, provided that the computational speed was increased [122]. For more examples of the early development of industrial applications of scatterometry the reader is referred to [26]. In the new millennium, a number of studies of in situ measurements using scatterometry were presented. With an infrared scatterometry system inside a deep reactive-ion etch instrument, Bosch-Charpenay et al demonstrated real-time etch-depth measurements of microelectromechanical systems [123]. In 2004 one of the few papers using one of the big commercial instruments presented a feasibility study for sub-nanometer in-line CD control [124]. For in situ resist trimming scatterometry has also been demonstrated as a valuable tool for controlling the profile of grating structures [125]. Today, scatterometry is widely used in the semiconductor industry and provides state-of-art measurements in the production lines of computer chips. With commercially available instruments, though out of reach for a normal research budget, the overlay accuracy can be measured with an uncertainty of less than 0.5 nm (k=3) [19]. The metrology demand from the semiconductor industry is continuously increasing. Some of the metrology challenges listed in the ‘International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors’ from 2013 are [126]: the need for integrated inline metrology tools to reduce scrap rate and increase product quality, reducing the size of test pattern areas, measuring on other materials than silicon, and measure on the always decreasing structures. Another field, where scatterometry has been used, is monitoring of crystal growth processes. The growth dynamics of nanowires fabricated using a bottom up approach is very complex and changes at all stages in the growth [127]. It has been demonstrated that monochromatic x-rays can be used for detailed analysis of the crystal formation rates of nanowires [128]. However, this requires large facilities, and monitoring using visible light is much easier to implement in a normal growth system [129]. With the improvements of nano-texturing surfaces in other materials than semi-conductors, scatterometry has found applications in fields from

The nanotextured injection molded bricks were fabricated using a steel insert patterned by nanoimprint lithography [130]. For fast characterization of the injection molded parts, the robust spectroscopic scatterometer described in section 2 was set up directly next to the heavy injection molding machine. Samples could be measured directly after fabrication and the scatterometer could be used to give direct feed-back to the injection molding parameters, e.g. injection pressure, hold time and temperature. The nano-textured surface of the samples was optimized to diffract as much light as possible into the ±1 orders, and therefore a high quality sample (steep side walls) generally exhibits lower diffraction efficiencies for the zeroth order than low quality samples (rounded corners).
6. Outlook
With many new products utilizing nano-textured surfaces entering the market, the demand for in-line metrology solutions for fast and accurate characterization in the nm-regime is rapidly increasing. As scatterometry is an ideal technique for quality control of mass production, some of the most obvious production methods for applying scatterometry are injection molding and R2R fabrication. However, before the industries begin to use scatterometry in the production lines at a large scale, several challenges have to be overcome. First of all, there is a need for more user friendly scatterometry solutions. At the moment scatterometry is for experts with the abilities to devise advanced models and experiments. With a
more user-friendly approach, more industries will probably begin to pick up the technology. In the project ‘Metrology for Highly Parallel Manufacturing’, part of the European Metrology Programme for Innovation and Research (EMPIR), the industrial applications of scatterometry, and several other promising technologies, are investigated [131]. It is the goal of the project to deliver faster and more accurate surface measurements to provide in-line feedback to large scale manufacturing processes. A second challenge is that scatterometry is a nonimaging technique. In general, the direct visualization when using an imaging characterization technology makes the uptake of the new technology much easier, as it can be conceptually difficult for an operator to validate measurements of surface topography without an image. As scatterometry is built on the principles of measuring the intensity of scattered light, it is easiest and most precise to use it on 1D or 2D gratings. Many products for the consumer market do not fulfill this. However, this can be overcome by using designated test areas with representative gratings. This technique has been used for many years in the print industry where the edge of a paper is used for testing and in the semi-conductor industry where scribe lines are used as test patterns. However, using test patterns requires a good understanding and correlation between the designated areas and the real structures of interest. Also the design of test areas has recently attracted interest for improvement of the precision of the measurement and at the same time reducing the target areas [132]. Correction of the line-edge of a grating is a similar perturbation that one needs to correct for [134, 135]. Another challenge for scatterometry is calibration of the instruments. The first steps towards a reference standard for scatterometry were described in 2012 [136] with focus on improving the tool-to-tool matching between scatterometers, CD-AFMs and CD-SEMs. The first data for such a reference standard was presented in 2015 [137] where a comparison of SEM, AFM, EUV scatterometry and Mueller polarimetry was made. These high quality samples should eventually be commercially available and pave the way for traceable scatterometry measurements. Here the tool-totool matching is important for lowering the combined uncertainties [139].
7. Summary
In this review, we have looked at the basic principles of scatterometry going from the development of the first scatterometers and software, to the future applications
of scatterometry. Scatterometry is based on an inverse modeling approach and the workflow for performing such measurements is essential. We have discussed the three steps in the workflow: the experimental measurements, the simulation of diffraction efficiencies and the comparison. A wide variety of scatterometers can be found and these can roughly be put into three groups: angular scatterometer, spectroscopic scatterometers and others, which include the hemispherical detectors. Experimental data has been presented for both angular and spectroscopic scatterometers in configurations targeting analysis of gratings with a pitch much longer than the wavelength of light (Γ?λ) and for gratings with a pitch comparable to the wavelength (Γ∼λ), respectively. Simulations of the diffraction efficiencies are preferentially performed using RCWA algorithms due to their high speed compared to FEM and FDTD and their multiple grating shapes compared to the simple scalar diffraction theory. As an example, RCWA has been used to perform simulations for finding the height, the filling factor and the sidewall angle of a 1D grating. For the comparison part, we have focused on two techniques: direct optimization and library search. Using direct optimization the diffraction efficiencies are calculated on the fly and selected free parameters are varied, e.g. using a differential evolution algorithm, in order to find a best match between model and data. For the library search approach, all values in a pre-generated database are compared to the experimental values. It can be time consuming to generate a large database, but once generated, the library search method is very fast and can be performed in a few ms. Two methods for uncertainty calculation have been presented in this review. In the uncertainty analysis for direct optimization a general least square analysis approach is presented that might be used to calculate both instrument and grating parameter uncertainties according to the GUM. The uncertainty analysis for library search method is much simpler and much faster but it can only be used for uncertainty calculation of the grating parameter. In the last decades, scatterometry has matured to be a fast, precise and accurate characterization technique. It has evolved from measurements of simple grating parameters in the 1990s to today’s in-line characterization of full wafers with sub-nm uncertainty. With more user-friendly scatterometers and software packages being developed, scatterometry is ready to be taken up by new industries such as injection molding and R2R companies.
