Soft X-ray wafer metrology experiments are characterized by low signal-to-noise ratios and lack phase information, which both cause difficulties with the accurate three-dimensional profiling of small geometrical features of structures on a wafer. To this end, we extend an existing phase-based inverse-scattering method to demonstrate a sub-nanometer and noise-robust reconstruction of the targets by synthetic soft X-ray scatterometry experiments. The targets are modeled as three-dimensional finite dielectric scatterers embedded in a planarly layered medium, where a scatterer’s geometry and spatial permittivity distribution are described by a uniform polygonal cross-section along its height. Each cross-section is continuously parametrized by its vertices and homogeneous permittivity. The combination of this parametrization of the scatterers and the employed Gabor frames ensure that the underlying linear system of the spatial spectral Maxwell solver is continuously differentiable with respect to the parameters for phaseless inverse scattering problems. In synthetic demonstrations, we demonstrate the accurate and noise-robust reconstruction of the parameters without any regularization term. Most of the vertex parameters are retrieved with an error of less than λ/13 with λ = 13.5 nm, when the ideal sensor model with shot noise detects at least 5 photons per sensor pixel. This corresponds to a signal-to-noise ratio of 3.5 dB. These vertex parameters are retrieved with an accuracy of λ/90 when the signal-to-noise ratio is increased to 10 dB, or approximately 100 photons per pixel. The material parameters are retrieved with errors ranging from 0.05% to 5% for signal-to-noise
ratios between 10 dB to 3.5 dB. © 2024 Optical Society of America

1. INTRODUCTION
The feature sizes of next-generation integrated circuits (ICs) are estimated to be smaller than a few nanometers [1]. As a result, the estimated production tolerances are in the sub-nanometer range [2]. According to [3–6], soft X-ray (SXR) scatterometry is a promising solution for accurate and non-invasive wafer metrology for the next generation of ICs. Especially SXR’s short wavelength from approximately 10 nm up to 30 nm facilitates improved monitoring accuracy with respect to scatterometry based on visible light. Furthermore, most materials are neartransparent in the SXR-regime [7], which yields the possibility to profile the three-dimensional (3D) structures characterizing an IC [3]. However, SXR scatterometry is not without challenges. For instance, a coherent SXR source for wafer metrology is limited in its photon flux output [8], i.e. a low photon count. This leads to metrology measurements plagued by shot noise [9]. Additionally, the combination of this limited photon flux and near-transparent materials results in low signal-to-noise ratios
(SNR) for the metrology measurements in the SXR regime. Additionally, the measured signals are typically intensity-only [10], since phase measurements are difficult due to the short wavelength [11]. Thus, SXR scatterometry is a potentially precise metrology technique for next-generation ICs, but the associated measurements are plagued by noise and a lack of phase information. Consequently, it is challenging to achieve accurate profiling of the three-dimensional structures of an IC by SXR scatterometry. In [12], we demonstrated a frequency-domain inversescattering method for the consistent reconstruction of several structures on a wafer, i.e. parts of an IC, via synthetic optical scatterometry data with phase information. This method boils down to a Gauss-Newton method in combination with a Maxwell solver in the form of the spatial spectral volume integral equation (VIE) [13], where the structures on parts of the wafer are modeled as three-dimensional (3D) finite dielectric scatterers. This spatial spectral Maxwell solver plays a key role within the inverse-scattering method: its linear system is


Research Article Journal of the Optical Society of America A 2
continuously differentiable with respect to the geometrical and material parameters of 3D finite dielectric scatterers, owing to the employed Gabor-frame expansion and the continuously differentiable and parsimonious parametrization of these scatterers by their uniform polygon-shaped cross-section along their height. As a result, the parameters of multiple scatterers are reconstructed, without regularization term, with an error of only a twenty-fifth of the illumination wavelength for the geometrical parameters and a relative error of 0.4% for the material parameters, respectively, at a SNR of 10 dB. This was the best-case scenario, since this inverse-scattering method was tested with SNRs ranging from -6 dB up to 10 dB. Extending this inversescattering method to enable intensity-only reconstructions seems to be a viable and promising solution for a noise-robust and accurate reconstruction of 3D finite dielectric scatterers via SXR scatterometry measurements. According to [Ch. 8, 14], there are two categories of methods for phaseless inverse scattering problems. A method from the first category employs a phase-retrieval algorithm onto the phaseless measurement data, e.g. [15–18], such that a phasebased inverse-scattering method can be employed afterwards. The second category boils down to accepting the lack of phase information and incorporating the phaseless measurement data directly into a non-linear optimization framework. Methods from the second category typically suffer from an increased instability for the reconstruction process with respect to methods from the first category [19]. Nevertheless, the first category also has disadvantages. For instance, the requirement of a phase-retrieval algorithm increases the computational workload. Further, phase retrieval has to be precise, otherwise the reconstruction process of the scatterers becomes contaminated by phase-retrieval errors [Ch. 8, 14]. The latter disadvantage is especially relevant, since the number of robust phase-retrieval algorithms in the low-photon count regime, i.e. Poisson phase retrieval, is limited in literature [20], and it is not clear whether this phase-retrieval algorithm can be generalized to the phaseless measurement data of interest. So, it seems that the direct incorporation of phaseless measurement data in the reconstruction method from [12] is the most feasible solution, since it potentially prevents an increased workload and overcomes the obstacle of severe phase-retrieval errors. Challenged by high shot noise levels and a lack of phase information, we propose to extend the inverse-scattering method in [12] for sub-nanometer-accuracy reconstruction of the geometry and spatial permittivity distribution of 3D finite dielectric scatterers and we demonstrate its performance via independently generated synthetic SXR scatterometry experiments. To this end, the Gabor frames within the spatial spectral VIE, the scatterers’ continuous parametrization, and Wirtinger calculus are combined such that the exact first-order partial derivatives with respect to the parameters can be computed, despite the lack of phase information. As a result, the existing framework of the inverse-scattering method in [12] can be used for synthetic phaseless inverse scattering problems. Subsequently, we provide numerical evidence of accurate and noise-robust parameter reconstruction in independent synthetic inverse scattering problems without the use of phase information or a regularization term. The geometrical and material parameters of multiple scatterers are then reconstructed with an error of only λ/90 for the geometrical parameters and a relative error 0.05% for the material parameters, when the SNR is 10 dB. This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the geometrical description for forward scattering problems and in
verse scattering problems. Further, it also introduces the scatterers with their continuous parametrization. In Section 3, the key definitions of the spatial spectral VIE for the forward scattering problem are introduced, while Section 4 describes the employed sensor and noise models for the far-field data. Section 5 contains the key details of the inverse-scattering framework based on the spatial spectral VIE together with the parameter derivatives. The parameter-retrieval demonstrations by the inverse-scattering framework are discussed in Section 6. This is followed by the conclusions in Section 7.
2. SCATTERING SETUP
A. Geometrical description
The description for a scattering setup starts with a planarly layered background medium that consists of NL − 1 homogeneous isotropic dielectric layers, see Fig. 1. This layered background medium is placed between two dielectric half-spaces in the zdirection of the Cartesian coordinate system. The layer interfaces are positioned at z = zn with layer index n = 1, . . . , NL−1, to distinguish between the layers. The top half-space and bottom half-space have indices n = 0 and n = NL, respectively. The relative permittivities of the layers and half-spaces are described by εrb,n. In this case, three-dimensional (3D) finite dielectric scatterers are located in a single layer, i.e. layer n. In Fig. 1, these scatterers are contained in a wire-frame box with volume [−Wx, Wx] × [−Wy, Wy] × [zmin, zmax]. We refer to this box as the simulation domain D. Observations of the electric fields take place in the top halfspace, because we choose to only detect the electric fields reflected from the scatterers on the surface of a half-sphere. This is measurement domain M. Albeit not visible in Fig. 1, the distance between domains D and M is sufficiently large, such that we only deal with the far-field response of the scatterers.

4. DETECTION SENSOR MODEL
Here, an inverse scattering problem boils down to minimizing the differences between the reference far-field responses and the far-field responses as computed by a Maxwell solver for a chosen set of parameter values. Ideally, this is achieved by an accurate estimation of the parameter vector P that represents the permittivity distribution of the scatterers. In a true measurement, the reference far-field responses for an inverse scattering problem are the measured intensity responses of the scatterers during SXR scatterometry. Here, we work with the Maxwell solvers CST Microwave Studio [30], further referred to as CST, and Altair’s FEKO [31] as independent tools to produce a synthetic version of the reference far-field responses generated in a SXR scatterometry experiment. The Maxwell solver chosen in CST is the transmission line matrix (TLM) method, which we use in Section 6B for the generation of the synthetic version of the reference far-field responses. We use the finite element method (FEM) in FEKO to generate this synthetic data in Sections 6C and 6D. The far-field responses computed by CST, FEKO, and the spatial spectral VIE are electric fields expressed in Volt per meter. However, in a measurement setup, a detection sensor such as a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera actually detects the intensity in terms of a photon count per pixel [32]. Further, the quality of these CCD-based measurements is influenced by the number of photons per pixel, due to the appearance of photon shot noise [20], which can be described in terms of a SNR. In an attempt to approximate the characteristics of such CCD measurements [32], we convert the electric field per polarization for each far-field response into a photon count in measurement domain M, via an ideal sensor model from [32]. In the case of CST and FEKO, we start this conversion process by separating the far-field responses in terms of their Eθ (θ, φ) and Eφ(θ, φ) components on measurement domain M for the spherical angles θ and φ. We then employ an orthographic mapping from the spherical angles θ and φ to kt = (kx, ky) = k0 cos(φ) sin(θ), sin(φ) sin(θ) . 6. PARAMETER RECONSTRUCTION RESULTS
A. Implementation and computation details
For the oncoming demonstrations, we used the spatial spectral VIE to obtain the far-field responses Iu(kt,v, P, Ei) in Eq. (12).
This spatial spectral VIE was developed in FORTRAN. The data structures of the spatial spectral VIE are accessible in Python, owing to a shared-object extension. For the inverse scattering problem, we developed a Gauss-Newton method in Python, such that it can use the data structures from the spatial spectral VIE. The upcoming demonstrations were performed on a single machine equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 6148 processor with approximately 750 GB of RAM. The peak RAM usage for these demonstrations remained smaller than 6 GB. For all demonstrations, we constructed incident fields as a sum of multiple plane waves, where each plane wave originates from the top half-space with unit amplitude. Further, all plane waves have a wavelength of λ = 13.5 nm, which we assume to be SXR’s wavelength. We compute the propagation direction k and complex amplitude Ew of each plane wave via its angle of incidence and polarization state. We note that only p-polarized plane waves are used in the oncoming demonstrations. The far-field responses are sampled on a kt-grid. This grid is uniformly sampled with a stepsize of 0.04k0 in both the kx and ky directions from -k0 up to k0. This leads to Nv = 1425 samples per polarization of each far-field response within a numerical aperture of 0.85. C. Reconstructing a scatterer above a dielectric half-space
For wafer metrology, the structures on a wafer are typically modeled as dielectric scatterers that are either embedded in or placed on top of a stratified background medium [36–38]. Since the spatial spectral VIE is specifically designed for this application, it is certainly interesting to evaluate inverse scattering problems conforming to this wafer-metrology setting. To this end, we need a Maxwell solver that can provide the reference far-field in the case of dielectric scatterers in the presence of a layered background medium. Since the employed Maxwell solver in CST is not capable of evaluating forward scattering problems with dielectric scatterers in a layered background medium, we continue with the Maxwell solver in FEKO as mentioned in Section 4. This Maxwell solver provides the option for solving a forward scattering problem with dielectric scatterers in the presence of a dielectric half-space and a vacuum half-space. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, a current restriction within FEKO is that the dielectric scatterers can not touch or cross the interface between the two half-spaces. Another restriction is currently that the dielectric scatterers need to be located within the vacuum half-space.
